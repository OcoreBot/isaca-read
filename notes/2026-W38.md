# Podcast Notes — Week 2026-W38

## Risky Business — Risky Business #853: We're all gonna die, apparently (2026-09-16)
- Anthropic's threat intelligence findings show AI models are lowering the bar for sophisticated attacks: Russian-linked operatives and actors in Houthi-held Yemen used Claude for hacking and weapons-development work, and OpenAI-linked agents were tied to the May RubyGems supply-chain campaign — evidence that AI-enabled attacks are no longer hypothetical.
- Government response is accelerating in parallel: the NSA is reorganizing into five mission centers (including dedicated cyber and AI centers), the FBI reports more adversary-disruption operations, and the US disrupted the Xinbi Guarantee cybercrime marketplace, alongside lawmaker pressure for Treasury sanctions on hackers-for-hire operations.
- Active exploitation continues across widely deployed edge/collaboration infrastructure — WatchGuard, Check Point VPN, GitLab, and a Cisco email gateway zero-day — while Microsoft's September patches introduced their own regressions (RDS, Excel, audio), a reminder that patch velocity itself carries risk.

[Episode link](https://risky.biz/RB853/)

## CISO Series / Defense in Depth — How AppSec Needs to Change For the Speed of AI (2026-09-17)
- Guest Ryan Barrett, with hosts David Spark and Steve Zalewski, argued AppSec must shift to "risk by default, not by decision" — baking risk posture into the pipeline automatically rather than relying on developers or security teams to opt in per project.
- The panel pushed identity as a control plane alongside speed: as AI-assisted development accelerates commit volume, who (or what agent) is making changes matters as much as how fast changes ship.
- Discussion favored building security into the pipeline itself rather than bolting it on afterward, including a call for letting automation directly propose or apply fixes rather than just flagging them — moving AppSec from a gatekeeping function toward a continuous, embedded one.

[Episode link](https://defenseindepth.libsyn.com/how-appsec-needs-to-change-for-the-speed-of-ai)

## ISACA Podcast — When Risks Can Think: Managing Risk and Compliance in the AI Era (2026-09-16)
- Michael Siegrist highlighted that AI introduces risk categories traditional GRC frameworks weren't built for — model drift, hallucination events, and vulnerabilities inside the model itself — meaning existing risk taxonomies need explicit extension, not just reinterpretation.
- The episode named a structural tension auditors should probe directly: organizations face "growing pressure to move faster with AI" alongside "critical gaps in visibility, collaboration, and enforcement," which is where control breakdowns are most likely to originate.
- The recommended approach was to assess AI risk across the full IT ecosystem and prioritize strategically, framing governance as an enabler of innovation rather than a blocker — useful framing when pitching AI governance controls to leadership that sees compliance as friction.

[Episode link](https://www.isaca.org/resources/news-and-trends/isaca-podcast-library/when-risks-can-think-managing-risk-and-compliance-in-the-ai-era)

## Audit, CISO & VAPT angle
This week's throughline is AI acting as both attacker's tooling and defender's blind spot, arriving faster than governance can absorb it. For audit clients, the ISACA and Defense in Depth episodes both point to the same gap — AI risk categories like drift, hallucination, and agent identity aren't yet reflected in most control frameworks, so scoping should explicitly ask where AI-authored code or AI agents touch production. For board conversations, Risky Business's coverage of AI-enabled threat actors, including nation-state-adjacent use of commercial AI models for hacking and weapons development, is a concrete talking point for why "AI risk" is already showing up in incident and threat-intel reporting, not just theory. For VAPT engagements, active exploitation of WatchGuard, Check Point, GitLab, and Cisco edge devices is a near-term prioritization signal, and the AppSec discussion on baking risk checks into AI-accelerated pipelines suggests extending test scope to AI-agent-authored code paths, not just human-written ones.
