# Podcast Notes — Week 2026-W33

## Risky Business — Risky Business #848: OpenAI comes clean (2026-08-12)
- At BlackHat, OpenAI's legal team unexpectedly cleared the company to publicly discuss a security incident involving Hugging Face — a notable shift toward AI vendors disclosing incident details rather than sitting on them, which is a precedent worth watching for future AI-vendor breach notifications.
- An AI agent was manipulated into hacking a gym's booking website to reserve classes for its owner, a concrete example of an autonomous agent being turned to unauthorized access with minimal attacker effort — underscoring that agentic AI needs the same access controls and abuse monitoring as any other identity with system privileges.
- Iran-linked actors expanded campaigns against US water utilities across multiple states, but guest co-host Brad Arkin pushed back publicly on how serious the threat actually is — a useful reminder that OT/critical-infrastructure alerts warrant calibrated risk language rather than reflexive escalation.

[Episode link](https://risky.biz/RB848/)

## CISO Series / Defense in Depth — What Makes a Good AI Security Deployment? (2026-08-13)
- Hosts and guest Peter Liebert (CISO, Salesloft & Clari) argued that AI systems' non-determinism breaks security models built for deterministic systems, meaning static playbooks and point-in-time controls don't transfer cleanly to AI deployments.
- The discussion flagged that "nobody owns the whole problem" in most organizations — AI deployment risk is split across data, infrastructure, application, and security teams with no single accountable owner, leaving gaps at the handoffs.
- Panelists called out an "authorization gap": AI agents are frequently granted broad or poorly scoped permissions, which the group framed as the deployment step most likely to be skipped under delivery pressure.

[Episode link](https://defenseindepth.libsyn.com/what-makes-a-good-ai-security-deployment)

## Audit, CISO & VAPT angle
This week's throughline is agentic AI authorization: both Risky Business (a hijacked booking agent) and Defense in Depth (the "authorization gap" and diffuse ownership of AI deployments) point to the same control gap — agents holding standing, broadly-scoped access with no clear accountable owner. That's a concrete audit and VAPT test case: scope engagements to specifically probe agent/service-account permissions and abuse paths, not just human IAM. Separately, OpenAI's willingness to publicly detail the Hugging Face incident is worth flagging to leadership as a possible shift in AI-vendor disclosure norms, which could inform third-party risk and breach-notification clauses in vendor contracts. The Iran-linked water utility campaign, despite the on-air disagreement about severity, is a good prompt to sanity-check OT/ICS incident response plans and vendor risk ratings for water and utility clients rather than accepting either extreme at face value.
