# Podcast Notes — Week 2026-W36

## Risky Business — #851: Agents are just ones and zeros, and tigers are just atoms (2026-09-02)
- Australian arrests of alleged TeamPCP hackers, credited partly to Brian Krebs's reporting, show law enforcement is now closing the loop on npm/supply-chain crews faster than in past years — a reminder that "the crew got arrested" is no longer a rare outcome worth downplaying in client threat models.
- OpenAI's debrief on the Hugging Face hack reportedly "raised more questions than it answers," underlining that even top AI labs are struggling to give clean incident narratives — a caution for auditors relying on vendor breach disclosures at face value.
- Hosts flagged voice-AI-agent-driven iPhone passcode phishing and infostealers now targeting Claude sessions specifically to drain paid usage, both signs that AI account credentials/sessions are becoming a distinct theft target requiring their own controls, not just generic endpoint hygiene.

[Episode link](https://risky.biz/RB851/)

## CISO Series / Defense in Depth — Recommendations to Reboot the Security Vendor Pitch (2026-09-03)
- CISOs Edward Contreras (Frost Bank) and Jill Rhodes (Option Care Health) argued vendors get roughly ten minutes to establish relevance, and pitches that lead with product tour instead of the buyer's specific pain lose the room immediately.
- Panel pushed "price the problem, not the product" — vendors (and by extension internal teams pitching tooling to leadership) should tie cost directly to a quantified business risk, or the ask won't survive budget scrutiny.
- Discussion on "whose meeting is it, really" is a useful lens for audit/GRC teams too: pitches (and audit findings presentations) fail when the presenter optimizes for their own narrative instead of the stakeholder's actual decision.

[Episode link](https://defenseindepth.libsyn.com/recommendations-to-reboot-the-security-vendor-pitch)

## Cloud Security Podcast — Why AI Won't Replace Your SOC: Federated Data & APEX Framework (2026-09-01)
- Nicole Beckwith (Cribl) argued classic IOC-based detection (hashes, IPs, domains) is effectively dead against AI-assisted attackers, pushing SOCs toward behavior/TTP-based detection as the durable control.
- Cribl's open APEX framework chains behavioral signals within time windows (e.g., recon-to-exploitation-to-lateral-movement inside 15 minutes) instead of maintaining thousands of per-technique rules — relevant for audit clients evaluating whether their detection engineering scales past MITRE ATT&CK checklist coverage.
- Key governance point: AI agents in the SOC must be provisioned as true, monitored identities rather than unmonitored service accounts — a concrete IAM control gap to test for in AI-tooling deployments.

[Episode link](https://www.cloudsecuritypodcast.tv/videos/gb-skip-navigation-search-create-avatar-image-why-ai-wont-replace-your-soc-federated-data-apex-framework)

## Audit, CISO & VAPT angle
This week's throughline is that AI-adjacent identities and sessions are becoming their own attack surface: infostealers draining Claude usage, AI agents needing "true identity" provisioning in the SOC, and OpenAI itself struggling to explain a Hugging Face-related incident cleanly. For audit clients, this argues for adding AI-agent and AI-account credential hygiene as an explicit scoping item rather than folding it into generic endpoint/IAM controls. For VAPT engagements, the shift from IOC-based to behavioral detection (APEX) means red-team reporting should increasingly demonstrate time-to-detection against chained TTP sequences, not just successful indicator evasion. For leadership conversations, the vendor-pitch critique from Defense in Depth doubles as a template for how security/audit teams should frame their own budget asks to the board: lead with quantified risk, not tooling features.
